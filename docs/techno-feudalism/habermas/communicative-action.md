# Communicative Action — the Positive Programme

> See [README.md](README.md) for orientation. This file sketches Habermas's later positive programme — communicative action, the ideal speech situation, public reason as procedural norm — and how it can serve as a yardstick for evaluating platform-mediated discourse.

Habermas's later work — primarily *Theorie des kommunikativen Handelns* / *Theory of Communicative Action* (1981; English translation 1984/1987 in two volumes), and his discourse-ethics writings (*Moralbewußtsein und kommunikatives Handeln* / *Moral Consciousness and Communicative Action*, 1983/1990) — develops the positive side of the project that the [1962 critique](refeudalization-1962.md) opens. Briefly relevant:

- **Communicative vs. strategic action.** Communicative action is oriented toward mutual understanding; strategic action is oriented toward influencing the other's behaviour (the distinction is the central architectural move of *Theory of Communicative Action*, Vol. 1). Platforms structurally favour strategic action — every interaction is shaped by reach optimisation, persuasion, performance. Communicative action requires conditions platforms do not provide.
- **The ideal speech situation.** Discourse aimed at understanding presupposes (counterfactually) that participants are free from coercion, equally able to speak, sincere, and oriented to validity claims rather than effects. The concept is developed in Habermas's earlier essays "Vorbereitende Bemerkungen zu einer Theorie der kommunikativen Kompetenz" (1971) and "Wahrheitstheorien" (1973), and is incorporated into *TCA* and the discourse-ethics writings. It is a regulative ideal, not a description — but it gives a precise account of what platform-mediated discourse systematically violates.
- **Public reason as a procedural norm.** Legitimacy comes from discourse meeting these conditions, not from any particular outcome (developed across Habermas's discourse-ethics writings and *Faktizität und Geltung* / *Between Facts and Norms*, 1992/1996). This is a strong basis for the claim that platform "town squares" are not in fact public spheres regardless of their scale.

## What this gives the techno-feudal argument

The positive programme is demanding. It does not give a ready-made tool spec. It does give a principled standard against which platform arrangements — and against which any proposed alternative — can be evaluated:

- "Does this tool widen or narrow the path off the platforms?" — useful but coarse.
- "Does this tool support communicative or strategic action?" — sharper. A tool that lets a creator publish independently *but only by performing for engagement metrics* still favours strategic action.
- "Does this tool's design approach the conditions of an ideal speech situation, or does it move further from them?" — sharpest. Few existing tools survive this test cleanly; the test is useful as a directional compass rather than a binary filter.

These criteria belong on the table when De Otio is making product-design decisions.
