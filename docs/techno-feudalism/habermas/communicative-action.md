# Communicative Action — the Positive Programme

> See [README.md](README.md) for orientation. This file sketches Habermas's later positive programme — communicative action, the ideal speech situation, public reason as procedural norm — and how it can serve as a yardstick for evaluating platform-mediated discourse.

Habermas's later work — primarily *Theorie des kommunikativen Handelns* / *Theory of Communicative Action* (Suhrkamp, 1981; trans. Thomas McCarthy, Beacon Press, Vol. 1 1984 and Vol. 2 1987), and his discourse-ethics writings (*Moralbewußtsein und kommunikatives Handeln*, Suhrkamp, 1983 [original orthography pre-1996 reform; not a typo] / *Moral Consciousness and Communicative Action*, MIT Press, 1990 [`translators to verify`]) — develops the positive side of the project that the [1962 critique](refeudalization-1962.md) opens. Briefly relevant:

- **Communicative vs. strategic action.** Communicative action is oriented toward mutual understanding; strategic action is oriented toward influencing the other's behaviour. On the most-cited reading this contrast is the load-bearing distinction of *TCA*; Habermas's full action-typology in Vol. 1, Ch. III is in fact four-fold (teleological/strategic, norm-regulated, dramaturgical, communicative) [`Vol. 1 Ch. III page refs to verify`], and the doc here deploys the simplified two-way contrast. Platforms structurally favour strategic action — every interaction is shaped by reach optimisation, persuasion, performance. On Habermas's terms, communicative action requires conditions platforms do not provide.
- **The ideal speech situation.** Discourse aimed at understanding presupposes (counterfactually) that participants are free from coercion, equally able to speak, sincere, and oriented to validity claims rather than effects. The concept is developed in Habermas's earlier essays "Vorbereitende Bemerkungen zu einer Theorie der kommunikativen Kompetenz" (in Habermas and Luhmann, *Theorie der Gesellschaft oder Sozialtechnologie*, Suhrkamp, 1971) and "Wahrheitstheorien" (in Helmut Fahrenbach, ed., *Wirklichkeit und Reflexion*, Neske, 1973) [`pagination to verify`], and is incorporated into *TCA* and the discourse-ethics writings. In the early/middle-period framing it is a "regulative ideal"; in his later work — especially *Wahrheit und Rechtfertigung* / *Truth and Justification* (Suhrkamp, 1999; trans. Fultner, MIT Press, 2003) — Habermas reframes these conditions as unavoidable pragmatic presuppositions of argumentation rather than a regulative ideal proper. The doc adopts the early/middle-period framing because it is what reads onto platform conditions most directly; the later reframing should be noted. We read the ISS as giving a precise yardstick against which platform-mediated discourse falls conspicuously short — that inference is itself a substantive interpretive move (anything short of the ISS "violates" it by design), not a direct entailment.
- **Discursive legitimacy as a procedural norm.** Legitimacy comes from discourse meeting these conditions, not from any particular outcome (developed across Habermas's discourse-ethics writings and *Faktizität und Geltung* / *Between Facts and Norms*, Suhrkamp, 1992; trans. Rehg, MIT Press, 1996). On Habermas's terms this is a strong basis for the claim that platform "town squares" are not in fact public spheres regardless of their scale. (Note: this file previously called the procedural norm "public reason," which is colloquially common but invites confusion with Rawls's specifically liberal-political sense — the two thinkers exchanged disputes about exactly this term in the *Journal of Philosophy* in 1995. We use "discursive legitimacy" or "the discourse principle (D)" to keep the dispute visible. The further question whether the Habermasian model of the public sphere is the right one — pressed by Fraser 1990 and by agonistic-pluralist critics — is taken up in [critiques.md](critiques.md).)

## What this gives the techno-feudal argument

The positive programme is demanding. It does not give a ready-made tool spec. It does give a principled standard against which platform arrangements — and against which any proposed alternative — can be evaluated:

- "Does this tool widen or narrow the path off the platforms?" — useful but coarse.
- "Does this tool support communicative or strategic action?" — sharper. A tool that lets a creator publish independently *but only by performing for engagement metrics* still favours strategic action.
- "Does this tool's design approach the conditions of an ideal speech situation, or does it move further from them?" — sharpest. Few existing tools survive this test cleanly; the test is useful as a directional compass rather than a binary filter.

These criteria belong on the table when De Otio is making product-design decisions.
