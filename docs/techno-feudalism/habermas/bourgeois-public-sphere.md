# The Bourgeois Public Sphere — What It Was

> See [README.md](README.md) for orientation. This file extracts Habermas's historical account of the *bürgerliche Öffentlichkeit* in *Strukturwandel der Öffentlichkeit* (1962; English: *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere*, trans. Burger & Lawrence, MIT Press, 1989).

Habermas's historical claim, in brief: between roughly the late 17th and late 18th centuries in Britain, France, and the German lands, a new social formation emerged between the private sphere (family, economic life) and the state. He calls it the *bürgerliche Öffentlichkeit* — the bourgeois public sphere.

## Constitutive features

- **A space of private individuals coming together as a public** to discuss matters of common concern. Coffeehouses in London, salons in Paris, *Tischgesellschaften* and reading societies in the German lands.
- **Disregard of status** in principle. Arguments were to be judged on merit, not on the speaker's rank. (Habermas was clear-eyed that this was a normative claim contradicted by who was actually admitted; the gap matters but does not invalidate the form.)
- **Rational-critical debate** as the normative mode. Not display, not negotiation of interests, not theatre — but the public use of reason in Kant's sense, on matters of common concern.
- **A press of opinion.** Periodicals, journals, pamphlets — not as channels of broadcast from above but as a medium of debate among readers who were also potential writers.
- **A presumed common interest** discoverable through reasoned discussion.
- **A check on state power.** Public opinion, formed in this way, became a source of political legitimacy that monarchs and ministries had to respond to.

## Material requirements

- A literate public.
- Economic independence sufficient for participation (the bourgeois qualifier is honest about whose participation it was).
- Leisure.
- Infrastructure: printing presses owned independently of the state, a postal system, physical gathering places, a market for periodicals.

## The link to the Enlightenment subject

The Enlightenment's individualist, autonomous-reasoning subject — Kant's *Sapere aude* citizen — *had a place to do that*. The public sphere was that place. Without a public sphere of this kind, the Enlightenment's normative individualism is a private virtue without a political function: one can reason for oneself but cannot bring that reasoning to bear on public affairs in any way that constitutes opinion-formation.

This is the load-bearing connection back to the [Enlightenment lineage](../enlightenment-lineage.md): the philosophical principles articulated there presupposed (often without saying so) a social formation of the kind described here. When the formation decays — see [refeudalization-1962.md](refeudalization-1962.md) — the principles persist as norms but lose their place to operate.
