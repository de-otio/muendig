# Enlightenment Lineage — Individualism and Anti-Feudalism

The Enlightenment is the intellectual movement that dismantled the legitimacy of the actual feudal order in Europe. If "techno-feudalism" names a real revival of feudal-shaped relations under new infrastructure, then the Enlightenment is the natural genealogy for the counter-argument. The thinkers below didn't anticipate algorithmic platforms, but they articulated the principles — autonomy, consent, natural rights, freedom of thought, the dignity of the individual against arbitrary authority — that an anti-feudal critique of platforms can inherit.

This file collects the thinkers and their relevant arguments, with honest notes on where the lineage transfers cleanly and where it doesn't.

## The thinkers

### Spinoza (1632–1677)

Pre-Enlightenment but a direct precursor on freedom of thought.

- **Key work:** *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus* / *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670). On the standard reading, especially of Ch. XX ("That in a free state every man may think what he likes, and say what he thinks"), Spinoza argued that the state cannot legitimately compel belief, that freedom of philosophising is essential to civil peace, and that authority over thought is incoherent because thought cannot in fact be commanded. (The "thought cannot be commanded" formulation is paraphrase, not a direct quotation.)
- **Anti-feudal force in its own time:** attacked the alliance of clerical and political authority that policed inner life.
- **Resonance with techno-feudalism:** the argument that authority over thought is incoherent translates poorly to authority over *expression* — platforms don't compel belief but they shape what can be said and seen at scale. Spinoza's frame is good for naming the offence; weaker for the algorithmic mechanism.

### John Locke (1632–1704)

The single most consequential anti-feudal voice in the early Enlightenment.

- **Key works:** *Two Treatises of Government* (1689). The First Treatise demolishes Filmer's *Patriarcha* (written c. 1630s–40s, published posthumously 1680) — the standard defence of hereditary monarchical authority by descent from Adam — line by line. The Second constructs the alternative: government by consent, legitimate only insofar as it protects natural rights to life, liberty, and property.
- **Core arguments relevant here:**
  - **Self-ownership.** "Every man has a property in his own person" (*Second Treatise*, §27). The same section continues with the labour-mixing argument — that one's labour, and what one's labour mixes with, becomes one's own — which the doc paraphrases below rather than quotes.
  - **Consent of the governed.** No legitimate authority without consent; consent that is not freely given is no consent.
  - **Right of revolution.** When authority systematically violates the trust on which consent rests, the governed may dissolve it.
- **Anti-feudal force in its own time:** delegitimised hereditary, divine-right, and patriarchal claims to rule.
- **Resonance with techno-feudalism:** Locke is the strongest source for the *consent* critique of platform ToS — clickwrap unilateral terms, enforced opaquely and changeable at the lord's discretion, are exactly the kind of pseudo-consent he rules out. Self-ownership extends naturally to a critique of the data-as-platform-property arrangement: if my labour and its product are mine by extension of self, the data extracted from my activity is too.

### Montesquieu (1689–1755)

- **Key work:** *De l'esprit des lois* / *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748). On the standard reading: separation of powers; intermediate institutions as protections against arbitrary concentration; the typology of regimes (republican, monarchical, despotic) by their animating principle. (Recent scholarship — e.g. Sharon Krause, Céline Spector — contests how cleanly Montesquieu maps onto modern separation-of-powers doctrine. The summary here is the textbook reading; see [`Krause and Spector — citation needed`].)
- **Anti-feudal force in its own time:** argued for institutional structure that resists concentration regardless of who concentrates it. Defended *intermediate* powers (parliaments, nobility) against absolute monarchy — a subtler position than pure individualism.
- **Resonance with techno-feudalism:** despotism for Montesquieu is rule by fear under arbitrary will. Algorithmic moderation that is opaque, unilateral, and unchallengeable fits the structural form. The *separation-of-powers* idea is harder to map: who holds judicial power against the platform? The DSA's out-of-court dispute settlement is a thin gesture in this direction.

### Voltaire (1694–1778)

- **Key positions:** religious tolerance, freedom of expression, anti-clericalism. *Treatise on Tolerance* (1763) on the Calas affair. The much-quoted line "I disapprove of what you say but will defend to the death your right to say it" is Hall's paraphrase, not Voltaire's words, but does fit the position.
- **Anti-feudal force in its own time:** the *infâme* he wanted écrased was the Catholic Church's coercive role in policing belief and expression — the religious face of the feudal-aristocratic order.
- **Resonance with techno-feudalism:** Voltaire's frame is moral-rhetorical, not structural. Useful for naming the offence of platform speech-policing and the legitimacy of pointed satire. Less useful for the infrastructural argument.

### Rousseau (1712–1778)

The most ambivalent figure for an individualist reading.

- **Key works:** *Discours sur l'origine et les fondements de l'inégalité parmi les hommes* / *Discourse on Inequality* (1755), *Du contrat social* / *The Social Contract* (1762). "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains" (*Social Contract*, Book I, Ch. 1, opening sentence; wording follows the standard English translations [`translator to verify`]). Distinguishes natural inequality (which is small) from artificial/political inequality (which is what causes the chains).
- **Anti-feudal force in its own time:** root-and-branch attack on the inherited social order as the source of artificial inequality and unfreedom.
- **Tension:** Rousseau's positive theory — the *volonté générale* / *general will* — has been read as both a deep defence of self-government and as a template for subordinating the individual to a collective. He is *not* a clean individualist; on the framing this file adopts, the Lockean lineage is closer to where techno-feudal critique wants to land. (This is a framing choice, not a settled reading: a republican / Pettit-style appropriation of Rousseau — see line on Pettit and Skinner below — would weight Rousseau more heavily than a Lockean appropriation does.)
- **Resonance with techno-feudalism:** the *Discourse on Inequality* genealogy — chains arising from accumulated artificial structure rather than from anyone's deliberate intention — is exactly the shape of the techno-feudal argument. The individual mechanisms are not malign; the cumulative structure is.

### Adam Smith (1723–1790)

Often misread as a partisan of pure markets; was in fact a direct critic of the *prior* economic order.

- **Key works:** *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). Attacked mercantilism, guild monopolies, primogeniture, entail, slavery, and the spirit of monopoly of merchants and manufacturers (the closest verifiable phrasing is *Wealth of Nations* IV.3.2: "the mean rapacity, the monopolising spirit of merchants and manufacturers, who neither are, nor ought to be, the rulers of mankind"; "wretched spirit of monopoly" as such is a paraphrase).
- **Anti-feudal force in its own time:** the system he was arguing *against* was the post-feudal mercantile-guild order with its inherited privileges, granted monopolies, and restraint of free movement and trade. "Natural liberty" was specifically liberty from those.
- **Resonance with techno-feudalism:** Smith's critique of monopoly transfers cleanly to platform monopoly and to the granted-privilege quality of platform access. His warning that "people of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public" (*Wealth of Nations*, Book I, Ch. X, Part II) is a fair description of incumbent-platform behaviour around regulation. Importantly, on what is now the dominant scholarly reading (Emma Rothschild, *Economic Sentiments*, 2001 [`page ref to verify`]; cf. Donald Winch, *Adam Smith's Politics*, 1978 [`page ref to verify`]), Smith's frame does *not* support the libertarian "leave platforms alone" reading — he saw monopoly and conspiracy as exactly what active institutional design must prevent. Libertarian Smith scholarship (e.g. James Otteson [`work to cite`]) reads the same texts more permissively; this file follows the Rothschild reading and flags the choice.

### Diderot, d'Alembert, and the Encyclopédistes (1751–1772)

- **Key project:** the *Encyclopédie* — the systematic compilation and public dissemination of knowledge across the arts, crafts, and sciences, against clerical and royal censorship.
- **Anti-feudal force in its own time:** treated knowledge itself as a public good and a leveller. Knowledge enclosed by guild, church, or court was knowledge weaponised for hierarchy.
- **Resonance with techno-feudalism:** the *Encyclopédie*'s spirit lives in Wikipedia, open-source software, the public web — and is exactly what platform enclosure is in tension with. The argument that knowledge accessibility is itself anti-feudal infrastructure is Diderot's argument, recast.

### Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

The most explicit philosophical articulation of Enlightenment-as-emancipation.

- **Key works:** *Beantwortung der Frage: Was ist Aufklärung?* / *What Is Enlightenment?* (1784), *Grundlegung zur Metaphysik der Sitten* / *Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals* (1785), *Kritik der praktischen Vernunft* / *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788).
- **Core arguments relevant here:**
  - **"Sapere aude!"** — Dare to know (*What Is Enlightenment?*, opening; the phrase itself is from Horace, *Epistles* I.2.40, deployed by Kant as the motto of Enlightenment). Enlightenment is the human being's emergence from "self-imposed minority" / *selbstverschuldete Unmündigkeit* — the inability or unwillingness to use one's own understanding without another's guidance. The minority is *self-imposed* when its cause lies not in lack of capacity but in lack of resolve and courage.
  - **Autonomy.** A rational being is one who acts on principles it gives itself; heteronomy (acting on principles given by another) is the structural form of unfreedom (*Groundwork*, Section II).
  - **The human as end-in-themselves.** Never to be treated as mere means (*Groundwork*, Section II — the Formula of Humanity, AK 4:429).
  - **Public use of reason.** The freedom to address the public as a scholar/citizen on matters of common concern is the defining freedom of an enlightened age (*What Is Enlightenment?*, second paragraph onward).
- **Anti-feudal force in its own time:** the most developed philosophical case for the moral and intellectual self-sufficiency of the individual against tutelage by church, state, and tradition.
- **Resonance with techno-feudalism:** unusually direct, though this is a reading *of* Kant's frame onto platform conditions, not something Kant's text directly licenses. The platform user is in a state of *induced* minority — knowledge of one's own audience, recommendation logic, content reach, and account status is held by the lord, not the user. Kant's "self-imposed" qualifier raises the uncomfortable question: how much of platform-tutelage is truly imposed and how much is accepted because the alternative is harder? The techno-feudal critique wants to recover Kant's *Sapere aude* without underestimating the structural cost of the exit. The end-in-themselves formulation also gives a sharp moral footing for opposing the user-as-data-feedstock arrangement.

### Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797)

- **Key work:** *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792). Argued that the exclusion of women from rational education and civil rights was an extension of feudal-patriarchal hierarchy, not a natural arrangement.
- **Anti-feudal force in its own time:** extended the Enlightenment individualist frame to the half of humanity it had quietly excluded — and in doing so showed the frame was honest only if applied universally.
- **Resonance with techno-feudalism:** the methodological move is the test — *who is excluded from the Enlightenment's promise in the current arrangement?* Apply that to platforms: who is structurally unable to exit, who is unable to publish independently, who is most fully a vassal? The answer is rarely the demographic the platforms' marketing addresses.

### Thomas Paine (1737–1809)

- **Key works:** *Common Sense* (1776), *Rights of Man* (1791), *The Age of Reason* (1794).
- **Core arguments relevant here:** hereditary government is absurd in principle and unjust in fact; rights inhere in persons regardless of state recognition; political legitimacy rests on present consent, not historical inheritance.
- **Anti-feudal force in its own time:** the most direct popular-language attack on hereditary privilege in the English-speaking world.
- **Resonance with techno-feudalism:** Paine's clarity about *hereditary* privilege transfers to the question of *granted* privilege — verified status, algorithmic boost, monetisation eligibility. The substance differs but the structural objection is the same: legitimacy by lord's grant, revocable at lord's will, is not legitimacy.

### The American founders, especially Jefferson (1743–1826)

- **Key text:** *Declaration of Independence* (1776). The bulk of the document is a list of grievances against arbitrary authority. The opening — "self-evident" rights, "consent of the governed," the right to alter or abolish governments that fail their purpose — is Locke condensed.
- **Anti-feudal force in its own time:** founded a state explicitly without a hereditary nobility and (formally) without established religion. Jefferson's later attacks on entail and primogeniture in Virginia were aimed directly at the legal residues of feudalism.
- **Resonance with techno-feudalism:** the Declaration's structural form — enumerate the abuses, name the breach of trust, claim the right to alter the arrangement — is a usable template. The deep moral compromise of the founders on slavery is the lineage's most serious integrity problem and must be acknowledged in any honest appropriation.

### John Stuart Mill (1806–1873)

Late and not usually classed as Enlightenment, but the direct heir.

- **Key works:** *On Liberty* (1859), *The Subjection of Women* (1869).
- **Core arguments relevant here:**
  - **Harm principle.** Mill's claim, in *On Liberty* Ch. I "Introductory", is — paraphrasing — that the only purpose for which power can rightfully be exercised over any member of a civilised community against their will is to prevent harm to others. (Direct quotation deferred — quote against a specific edition before re-introducing quote marks; cf. *On Liberty and Other Writings*, ed. Stefan Collini, Cambridge UP, 1989 [`page ref to verify`].)
  - **Tyranny of the majority.** In the same chapter, Mill argues that social tyranny is more formidable than many kinds of political oppression because it leaves fewer means of escape and penetrates more deeply into private life. (Wording paraphrased; the doc previously stitched two near-quotations into one — direct quotation deferred until checked against a named edition.)
  - **Individuality as a component of well-being.** Conformity is a loss not only to the individual but to society's capacity to discover better ways of living (*On Liberty*, Ch. III "Of Individuality, As One of the Elements of Well-being").
- **Anti-feudal force in its own time:** developed individualism into a positive vision of human flourishing, not merely a defence against state authority.
- **Resonance with techno-feudalism:** Mill's *social* tyranny insight is the most directly transferable. Platforms don't exercise state-style political power; they exercise something closer to Mill's social tyranny — penetrating into the details of life, shaping what can be said and seen, "enslaving the soul" by colonising attention. *On Liberty*'s defence of individuality against conformity translates to a defence of independent publication against algorithmic homogenisation.

## Cross-cutting themes

Pulling out the through-lines that the techno-feudal argument can inherit:

1. **Authority requires consent, and consent requires real alternatives.** Locke, Paine, Jefferson. Clickwrap ToS with no real exit are not consent.
2. **Self-ownership extends to the products of one's activity.** Locke. Data as platform-property is hard to defend on Lockean grounds.
3. **Autonomy is the structural form of freedom.** Kant. Heteronomy — acting on principles given by another, including by an algorithm — is the structural form of unfreedom regardless of how comfortable it is.
4. **Knowledge accessibility is anti-feudal infrastructure.** Diderot. Enclosure of knowledge is itself a feudal move.
5. **Monopoly and granted privilege are the enemies of liberty, not its products.** Smith. The libertarian misreading of Smith does not survive contact with what he actually wrote.
6. **Social tyranny is at least as serious as political tyranny.** Mill. Platform power is closer to social than political.
7. **The integrity test of any individualist frame is who it excludes.** Wollstonecraft. Apply this to platforms: whose vassalage is most complete?
8. **Cumulative structures can chain people without anyone deliberately chaining them.** Rousseau (*Discourse on Inequality*). Exactly the shape of the techno-feudal mechanism.

## Where the lineage doesn't transfer cleanly

Honest gaps to think through:

- **The Enlightenment thinkers wrote against authority that was personal, named, and located.** A king, a bishop, a landlord. Platform authority is corporate, partly algorithmic, distributed across jurisdictions, and often genuinely *no one's* fault in the sense earlier philosophers would recognise. The vocabulary of personal tyranny doesn't quite fit; the vocabulary of structural tyranny (Marx, Foucault, more recent) may transfer better for that piece.
- **The Enlightenment took for granted a common public sphere.** Habermas's *Strukturwandel der Öffentlichkeit* / *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962; English: trans. Thomas Burger with the assistance of Frederick Lawrence, MIT Press, 1989) is the obvious bridge text — he argued the bourgeois public sphere the Enlightenment depended on had already eroded by the 20th century. Platforms have replaced rather than restored it. The Enlightenment frame assumes a public the techno-feudal arrangement no longer reliably produces. The Habermas account is itself contested: see Nancy Fraser, "Rethinking the Public Sphere" (*Social Text* 25/26, 1990) and the [Habermas critiques](habermas/critiques.md) for the standard challenges, none of which dissolve the bridge but all of which sharpen it.
- **Negative liberty is necessary but insufficient.** "Free from interference" doesn't help if the infrastructure for independent action doesn't exist. The techno-feudal critique needs a positive-liberty supplement (Isaiah Berlin, "Two Concepts of Liberty," 1958 inaugural lecture at Oxford). The Enlightenment lineage tilts toward negative liberty; the constructive part — building anti-feudal *infrastructure* — needs help from elsewhere.
- **The Enlightenment's record on universality is real but partial.** Slavery, colonisation, the exclusion of women, class-bound assumptions about who counts as a rational citizen. Wollstonecraft, Frederick Douglass, and later thinkers extend the frame; an honest appropriation acknowledges the work was incomplete and is still incomplete.
- **The "rational individual" the Enlightenment posits is partly a construction the platforms now actively erode.** Attention, deliberation, and reasoned judgement are exactly what the engagement-optimising algorithm preys on. Defending the Kantian autonomous subject requires defending the conditions for that subject's emergence. (Note that this move — treating autonomy as socially produced rather than as a noumenal capacity given with rationality itself — is a critical-theoretic / Habermasian supplementation of Kant, not Kant's own implication. A strict Kantian reading would resist it; the doc adopts the supplementation deliberately.) This is a stronger and more demanding programme than simply citing Kant.

## Open questions for further development

- Which thinkers in the *continental* lineage extend the structural critique where the Enlightenment vocabulary runs out — Marx on alienation, Weber on rationalisation, Foucault on disciplinary power, Habermas on the public sphere, Arendt on the social? Worth a sibling file.
- Is there an honest reading of the Enlightenment that handles the universality failures without dissolving into a separate "decolonial / critical" tradition? Or is the right move to acknowledge the Enlightenment as one inheritance among several?
- The contemporary "republicanism" revival (Philip Pettit, *Republicanism: A Theory of Freedom and Government*, 1997; Quentin Skinner, *Liberty before Liberalism*, 1998) — freedom as non-domination rather than non-interference — is a sharper fit for platform critique than either pure liberalism or pure libertarianism. Worth its own treatment.
- For De Otio specifically: which of these thinkers does the org want to claim as orienting? "Kantian autonomy + Lockean consent + Smith on monopoly + Mill on individuality" is a defensible quartet but it is a *choice*, not a given.
